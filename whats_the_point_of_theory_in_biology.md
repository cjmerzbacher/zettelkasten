What role does theory play in the sciences?
- Theory as the end goal of science - experiments used to build up a theory
But what if theory is just another tool for scientific reasoning?
- Explanation - why a model behaves as it does by deriving general properties (stability, conservation)
- Interpolation - finding parsimonious frameworks to connect many data points in a unified way
- Extrapolation - making predictions of things not in current data
Much theory exists in a bubble with only marginal impacts on engineering (possibly due to simulation)
- ML changes this: if data is not interpretable through these models, what will offer insight into reality?
Systems biology aimed to put together data-driven models of biological processes to uncover design principles
- But many big names have pivoted to method development
- Nature of data too coarse-grained to generate first-principles models
Are there things in biology we can only get from classical theory, not large statistical models?
- Top-down vs. bottom-up
- Bottom-up (Covert) - build virtual cell from mechanistic equations and first principles
- Top-down looking for patterns in data
> My worry with purely data-driven models is that we lose something special that is inherent in science itself. If we’re only probing a model in the context of making useful predictions or finding a cure for some disease, will we actually be asking the right questions to understand the fundamental nature of a cell? Will we even know how to wield these sparse autoencoders, or which questions to ask of them, if we only ever approach this problem from the top-down?

Skeptical of bottom-up modelling because once you are not fully certain about mechanisms or parameters, the model does not generalize.
Start from simple processed and nail down parameters (with uncertainty), predict new data, perturb, and see if the predictions are still accurate. Pushes away from mechanistic models towards phenomenological ones.

> And then we have machine learning, where theory and experiments have entered this wildly virtuous flywheel, such that every marginal conceptual advancement immediately seems to produce practical improvements and drive new engineering work. Good researchers are worth astronomical sums of money just to keep the flywheel spinning. It’s probably at least somewhat a bubble, but the labor market tells us something about how productive researchers can be in the ideal circumstances.
