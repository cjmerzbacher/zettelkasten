## Notes

I begin this project from a fundamentally narcissistic place; perhaps that is why I will be able to finish it. I decded to move abroad in January of 2020, although I dreamed of it enough in the years before that to apply for a Fulbright grant to New Zealand in 2019. But I know the night when consideration became certainty, necessity. I have the journal entry. I left America in August of 2021, but as you will learn in these pages, I have kept leaving it, have considered and reconsidered returning through the looking glass.

I am interested in how one makes na artistic life, a creative life, a fulfilled one - in exile from empire. False banishment or self-imposed hiatus. What is it like to choose to leave America, and yet be drawn by it ever closer in one's work?
                 
I have been trying to figure out how people live as artists and writers and Americans abroad. It is vital that I learn this - it is my route into life. I could not see a path forward for myself in America; it feels melodramatic but not inaccurate to say it was necessary to leave to save my own life. I had to find a place I could create myself and my art. Amsterdam is that place, at least for the foreseeable future. And so now that I am here - how do I live?

## Draft

> "What he sought was always lying ahead, and even if it was a matter of the past it was a past that changed gradually as he advanced on his journey, because the traveler's past changes according to the route he has followed: not the immediate past, that is to which each day that goes by adds a day, but the more remote past. Arriving at each new city, the traveler finds again a past of his that he did not know he had: the foreignness of what you no longer are or no longer possess lies in wait for you in foreign, unpossessed places." -- Italo Calvino, *Invisible Cities*

In his book *Hyperobjects: Philosophy and Ecology after the End of the World*, Timothy Morton introduces the titular concept as an entity distributed massively in both time and space relative to humans. Climate change, capitalism, or the deployment of a technology like AI are all hyperobjects; they extend themselves across many dimensions, tampering with causality, absorbing other objects, forcing their effects on the things and people around them. 

I have been trying to write about leaving America. 

It feels impossible, perhaps because I have always found it challenging to write about experiences while still inside them; but I imagine I will be reckoning with this particular departure for the rest of my life; whatever happens, I can never return to being the person who never left. But if I am to remain in this hyperobject for life, then I may as well start trying to interpret it. 

I left America in August of 2021, because I felt I could no longer survive there with my empathy intact. That is melodramatic, perhaps, but true, as is the statement that leaving was the hardest and best thing I have ever done. I will not turn this essay into a litany of the United States' sins, or claim that Europe is free from them, but I will say that I felt then, and still feel, that I could not feel the world deeply, maintain any level of mental stability, and remain. I wanted to be a successful scientist, an artist and writer, and a happy person, and when I looked forward at my future there, I felt nothing so much as a great and yawning dread.

So I packed my life into three suitcases, took multiple COVID tests, and boarded a flight to Frankfurt. I was moving to Scotland, and I was done with the States. *I might not ever come back*, I told friends. *Fuck America.* 

The United States is, in many ways, impossible to leave. Certainly, it has borders, but at least in Western Europe, it's influence and power remain inescapable. As a scientist, much of the field I work in is based in the US, either at institutions there or through international grants. Even in the private sector, much investment flows through and around the US; in response to its VC trends, its consumer market, and its government policies. The internet - especially the Anglophone internet - is heavily Americanized. Try, for example, looking for financial advice, and you'll find most posts on "general" forums are US specific without admitting it. I won't bore everyone with a discussion of taxes, but suffice it to say the US has a unique citizenship-based tax regime that means I am held to higher financial reporting requirements, which means many European banks refuse to do business with me. If you fly through the Dublin airport to the U.S. you'll actually enter the country in a special chunk of the airport. You might think you're still on Irish soil, but legally, you've entered the American hyperobject once more. 

Even though it is language cribbed from a philosophy text, "hyperobject" feels at home in the genre of science fiction. It evokes leviathan scale and deep time; it reminds me of the grandeur of the opening Star Wars crawl or of the dizzying feeling of trying to grasp a difficult mathematical concept. Science fiction is one of the few art forms, to me, that can capture the strangeness and scale of this experience; the way it feels simultaneously vast and claustrophobic; the way the laws of physics "back home" seem to not apply to the beings, places, or spacetime one now occupies. 

In her novel *The Disposessed*, Ursula K. Le Guin follows a physicist from the anarchist world of Anarres, Shevek, as he leaves the planet and journeys to its twin world, Urras. Hundreds of years previously, Anarres was settled by refugees from Urras's economic system who aimed to create a society without hierarchy, and they have mostly succeeded. The planet has been nearly completely cut off from outside contact as a way of maintaining the revolution; though Shevek has been sending his scientific work for publication to Urras for years, he is little prepared for the culture shock of Urrasti society.

The subtitle of the book is "An Ambiguous Utopia", and Le Guin lives up to that promise. Anarres is not perfect; Shevek faces friction from his professional colleagues and separation from his partner and child by the communal job assignment system. But neither does it suffer from the "moral claustrophobia" of Urras: cities made of "huge shining boxes of stone and glass, immense, ornate, enormous packages, empty, empty." 

Shevek always plans to return to Anarres, and that desire structures his entire experience of the twin planet. "He would always be one for whom the return was as important as the voyage out. To go was not enough for him, only half enough; he must come back." But he also recognizes the reciprocality of all journeys there and back again. His trip to Urras is it's own kind of return:

 > The Odonians who left Urras had been wrong, wrong in their desperate courage, to deny their history, to forgo the possibility of return. The explorer who will not come back or send back his ships to tell his tale is not an explorer, only an adventurer. He was not part of [Urras]. Nor was he part of the world of his birth.

Of course, by leaving, you are defining yourself *in contrast to*, which merely reinforces that element of your identity. I remember saying, in those first months of Scotland, that being American had been far, far down the list of traits I identified with before the move, and now it was near the top. James Baldwin, upon his own force-ejection from the United States, saw that black and white Americans in Paris had the same feeling of being "divorced from their origins": "It became terribly clear in Europe, as it had never been here, that we knew more about each other than an European ever could." Ben Lerner, in *Leaving the Atocha Station*, puts it even more incisively:

> Each member of this...network resented the others, who were irritating reminders that nothing was more American, whatever that means, than fleeing the American, whatever that is, and that their soft version of self-imposed exile was just another of late empire's packaged tours.

Especially as a student abroad, the university town I had moved to was full of other Americans on their master's year abroad in the UK. I met and befriended plenty of these Americans, but often felt as if we were speaking across a gap. What had felt like a fundamental rupture in my life was for them an interlude before a return home, "a year cast out of the line of years". Even when spring came, and brought with it pangs of homesickness so strong I woke with them in my throat each morning, I did not know how to return. 

The philosopher L. A. Paul writes in her book *Transformative Experience* of life choices that change us so fundamentally that "we can't know what our lives will be like until we've undergone the new experience." Furthermore, undergoing these "transformative" experiences changes our desires, our identities enough that we cannot make the decision based on who we are now. 

I wanted to return, I did not want to, I wanted to, in turns. But regardless of what I wanted, moving back to America felt impossible. Another gap, this one even larger and entirely unbridgeable. What I wanted had been changed by the departure: my sense of the order of adulthood, my definition of a good life. I could not imagine dragging the wheelie trash cans for my house out to the curb every Tuesday. I could not imagine spending hours a week in a car. I knew, that if I finished my degree and returned, these things would be around me again, normal. But I knew I would not return, not the self that had begun the transformation of leaving. Walking back is just another portal. Who knew what version of me lay on the other side?
